Executive Summary

The game's combat foundation is genuinely strong: five levels of escalating faction warfare, seven bosses with real mechanical identity, a four-class enemy taxonomy with its own audio language, and a polished feel layer (silhouette hit flashes, muzzle flashes, class-signature soundscape). What separates it from "great" is almost entirely structural, not mechanical: everything the player earns is random, temporary, and disconnected. Score means nothing. Upgrades arrive by luck and vanish at death. Every run is the same five levels in the same order, and the excellent lore (Controlled Humans, the Void's two faces, the Defender invasion) is locked away in menu guides instead of being told through play.

The three flagship changes: make points a spendable currency, make weapon growth a series of player choices instead of lucky drops, and let the story happen inside the levels rather than beside them.

1 · The Gameplay Loop — moment to moment

Right now the loop is: hold fire, weave, collect whatever drops, survive until the boss. It works, but the player is never asked to make an interesting decision mid-wave. Recommendations, in order of impact:

  • Combo / multiplier meter. Kills within ~2s of each other build a multiplier (×1 → ×5) that decays on idle and resets when you take a hit. Instantly, every second of play has stakes beyond survival: play aggressively to keep the chain, play safe and lose income. This single system converts the existing score into moment-to-moment motivation — and it feeds directly into the currency economy in §4.
  • Graze reward. Bullets that pass within a few pixels of the ship pay a small multiplier bump and a spark effect. It rewards exactly the behaviour that makes shmups thrilling — flying toward danger — and it costs nothing to lose. (The near-miss detection is cheap: one distance check on bullets already iterated.)
  • A panic button. The genre-standard bomb: one screen-clearing Void Pulse with 2–3 stocks per level. Deaths to overwhelming bullet walls currently feel unfair; a bomb converts them into "I misjudged my resources", which is the difference between quitting and one-more-run. Ties into lore trivially (captured Void tech, unstable, hence limited).
  • Mini-objectives inside levels. Once or twice per level, a timed optional event: "a Defender convoy is crossing — destroy the escort in 20s for a guaranteed curved-gun drop", "a Void rift is opening — close it before it finishes spawning". The phase system already exists; these give phases identity beyond spawn-table changes.
  • Hit feedback asymmetry. Player damage currently reads similarly whether it's 5 HP or 40 HP. Scale screen shake, flash and a short low-pass audio duck with damage size so big hits feel big and chip damage stays quiet.

2 · Weapon Systems & Advancement

Current state: main gun (firepower 1–5), rapid fire (timer), tracking guns (elite drops), curved gun (1–3), all acquired by random drop luck, stacking on top of each other, and all lost on death. Every maxed-out ship ends up flying the same loadout, because the game never asks "which weapon do you want to be flying" — it only ever asks "do you want more of what you already have."

Why the obvious fix doesn't work here

The first idea anyone reaches for is a choice popup: when a drop lands, slow time and offer two options, pick one with a keypress. That gives the player a decision, but not a ship that plays differently from run to run — and it runs straight into the constraint that matters most in this genre: anything that pauses the action, even for a second, to show boxes to click is a tax on a game about never looking away from the bullets. A choice screen every 20 seconds is a choice screen the player starts resenting by level 3.

The real problem is harder and more interesting: let the player actively pick which weapon they're flying, let them change their mind mid-run, and don't let switching become a free respec to full power — without ever taking their hands off the controls or their eyes off the screen.

The mechanism: separate "owned" from "equipped"

The trick that makes this work without any menu is to stop treating weapon power as one number that belongs to the player, and instead give each weapon its own level, tracked independently, whether or not it's currently active. Only one of them fires at a time — that's the loadout — and a single keypress swaps which one is live, instantly, with no pause and no screen.

That one change answers both halves of the problem at once:

  • Switching is free to do, but not free in effect. Tapping a hotkey to swap from Laser to Missiles is instant — but if the player has been feeding every pickup into Laser all run and never touched Missiles, swapping over hands them a level-1 Missile gun, not a maxed one. The power sits with the weapon, not the player, so there's a real reason to commit to one thing for a while instead of flicking between all of them constantly.
  • Changing your mind is always available and never a wasted decision. If a level throws a bullet pattern that a piercing gun handles better than a spread gun, the player can switch immediately — they just won't be at full strength on it yet. That's a trade-off made in the moment, not a choice locked in ahead of time.

How the switch itself should feel

None of these need a menu — each is just an input, the same category of action as firing or moving:

  • Dedicated keys per weapon (1/2/3). Most direct — press the number for the weapon you want live. Works best once the player owns 3–4 weapon types and wants to jump straight to a specific one.
  • One cycle key (Q or Tab). Steps to the next owned weapon in order. Fewer keys to teach and easier to reach one-handed, at the cost of sometimes stepping past the one actually wanted.
  • A charging selector, Gradius-style. A small strip of icons in the HUD; a special pickup nudges a highlight along the strip, one button commits whichever weapon is currently lit. More thematic (could be reskinned as unstable "Void tech"), and adds its own small moment of "wait for the right one, then commit" — but it's one more idea to explain before it clicks for a new player.

All three keep the actual swap to a single keypress with nothing on screen changing but the HUD icon. Start with dedicated number keys — it's the least ambiguous — and only look at the cycling or charging versions later if playtesting shows the number-key mapping feels cluttered.

Guarding against switch-spam

Because the swap itself is free, a player could in principle flick between weapons every half-second to dodge whatever a specific bullet colour "counters." If that shows up in testing, the fix doesn't need to touch the core system: a short 1–2 second cooldown between switches, or a brief power ramp-up on the weapon just switched to, both discourage spamming without ever putting a menu back in the way.

Where the extra weapon variety comes from

Right now there are really only two "feel" categories in the game — a straight gun and a curved/homing gun — which isn't enough for a slot system to mean anything; swapping between two similar guns isn't an interesting choice. The weapon-tree ideas already floated for this game are the right raw material: turn Lance (piercing, narrow), Storm (wide spread), Hunter (homing missiles) and Orbit (defensive ring shots) into four genuinely distinct primary weapons instead of end-of-run branch choices. Most of the projectile behaviour for these already exists in some form in the current gun, tracking, and curved-shot code — the work is separating them into standalone weapons with their own pickup and their own level, not inventing new projectiles from nothing.

Pickups should be colour- and shape-coded per weapon, so a player can tell "that's a Lance pickup" at a glance, the same way enemy bullets are already read by colour. Choosing what to grab — or which weapon to lean into — becomes a read made in a fraction of a second while flying, not a decision that needs explaining.

What stays as-is

Not everything needs folding into the slot system. Rapid fire reads naturally as a fire-rate buff that applies to whichever weapon is currently equipped, rather than being a weapon of its own. A defensive layer — shield, or a fixed always-on secondary like a light curved-shot — can stay separate and non-swappable, so the player never drops to zero capability while experimenting with the primary slot.

Softening death, revisited

With per-weapon levels, "lose everything on death" becomes "lose progress on several weapons independently" — which is actually easier to make forgiving: drop every owned weapon by one level rather than wiping the whole loadout, so a death costs real ground without deleting an entire run's worth of decisions.

  • Overdrive instead of flat rapid-fire. Still stands on its own regardless of the above: replace the rapid-fire timer with a heat/overdrive bar charged by kills, giving a burst of drastically boosted fire on demand. Player-activated power beats passively-timed power either way.

3 · Sense of Progress

  • Within a run: the ship should visibly change as it grows. Turret models for tracking/curved already exist — extend that: firepower changes bullet visuals per tier (already partially true), overdrive adds an engine glow state, max-build ships should look dangerous. Add a phase banner ("PHASE 4 — THE FLEET ARRIVES") using the GET READY splash machinery so mid-level escalation is announced, not just felt.
  • Across runs: a persistent pilot profile in localStorage (the mixer already persists settings, so the plumbing exists): total kills per enemy type, best level reached, best score per difficulty, boss kill counts, deaths by cause. One "Records" screen off the title menu. Cheap, and it makes every run leave a mark.
  • Medals. 15–20 named achievements checked at run end (no-damage boss, 100% guide unlocked, beat L3 without bombs, ×5 chain for 60s). Displayed on the Records screen; each medal can also gate an unlock (§5).
  • End-of-level report card. Between levels, a 3-second summary: kills, accuracy, peak chain, damage taken, grade (D–S). Grades create self-set goals and give the between-level shop (§4) a natural home.

4 · Use of Points

Score currently does nothing but sit in the HUD — the single biggest untapped system in the game. Convert it into the spine of the whole experience:

  • Score = currency ("Salvage"). Between levels, a 3-item shop: repair (health), one weapon-tree step of your choice, +1 bomb stock, shield charge, or bank it. Suddenly every point earned in combat is a decision deferred — and the combo/graze systems from §1 become an economy, not just bragging rights.
  • Continues cost points. On death after the checkpoint: "Continue from checkpoint — 5,000 salvage" (scaling per use). Skilled play literally buys survivability; hoarding vs spending becomes a strategy axis.
  • High-score table. Local top-10 per difficulty with date and level reached. The game already has the polish level where players will chase this; it costs one localStorage array and one screen.
  • Chain-banked bonuses. End-of-phase payout = base points × peak chain that phase, presented with a satisfying tally sound. Makes the multiplier legible and celebratory.

5 · Unlocks

  • Ship variants (unlocked by medals/levels, chosen at difficulty select): Vanguard (default), Wraith (faster, 75 HP, starts with curved 1), Bastion (slower, 125 HP + shield start, weaker gun). Different starting loadouts multiply replay value against the same content for near-zero new content cost.
  • Challenge modes from existing machinery: the sandbox already spawns any boss — a Boss Rush mode (all 7 back-to-back, shared health pool, leaderboard entry) is mostly menu work. Similarly a Gauntlet (endless waves from the existing spawn tables, difficulty ramping every 90s).
  • Guide entries as collectibles. The enemy/boss guides are already excellent — lock each entry until the enemy has been killed once (silhouette + "???" before). Turns the codex into a collection drive and rewards using the guides. Kill-count tiers (10/100) can add flavor text per entry.
  • Earned cosmetics, not stats: engine trail colors, bullet tints, victory emblems for hard-mode clears — visible in-game and on the records screen.

6 · Variety

  • Champion variants. A small % of any enemy spawns as a named champion: tinted, 3× HP, one modifier (splits on death / drops a bomb stock / shielded until close range). One system, applied to 20+ existing enemy types, yields hundreds of encounter permutations — the cheapest variety multiplier available.
  • Level events ("Void weather"). Per-phase ambient modifiers announced by banner: meteor crossfire, void fog (reduced visibility, enemies emerge closer), Defender ceasefire (Defenders ignore you for 30s and fight the Void — showcase the three-way war!), gravity eddies that curve all bullets. Two or three per level, randomized, so runs stop being identical.
  • Branch choice at level end. After the report card: "Route A — asteroid belt (dense hazards, +50% salvage) / Route B — patrol corridor (safer, standard)". Even two short alternate phase-tables per level create the feeling of a campaign map.
  • Promote the candidate enemies. Twelve finished sandbox-only enemies (aegis, minelayer, breacher, phantom…) are already built and tested — graduating even six of them into late-level spawn tables and champion pools is free content.

7 · Story

The lore is already unusually good for an arcade shmup — four factions with a real geometry between them (humans enslaved, Void corrupting, Void Pure invading, Defenders arriving with their own agenda in L5). The problem is that it's all in menus. Move it into the run:

  • Mission briefings. Three lines of fiction on the GET READY screen per level (it now exists and already holds the player for a beat): who you're flying against, why, one hook. Zero new UI needed.
  • Wingman barks. A single character — a comms officer — delivering one-line subtitles at scripted beats: first dreadnought ("That's a carrier. Kill the escorts first."), Defender arrival ("Unknown fleet — they're not shooting at the Void… wait, they're not shooting at us either."), Gatekeeper discovery. 10–15 lines total; text-only; enormous personality return.
  • Boss title cards. One second, name + epithet: "THE KNIGHT — Oathbreaker of the Third Fleet". The bosses have mechanical identity; give them narrative identity for free.
  • An actual ending scene beyond the victory screen: three beats of text over the starfield after the Machine falls, and a post-Gatekeeper stinger that names the next threat — sequel hook for the wider Void Series.
  • Environmental storytelling. The background system (planets, nebulae) can carry story: L2's planet cracked and venting after the boss; Defender wreckage drifting in L5 backgrounds; the Machine's level sky darkening as its health falls (the corruption tint already exists).
